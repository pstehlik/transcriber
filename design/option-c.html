So the thing that really struck me about the early days was how quiet everything was. You'd walk into the office, and there were maybe six of us, all hunched over these ancient ThinkPads, and nobody spoke before noon. It was almost like a library. But the energy was incredible, you know? Everyone was building something they believed in.

I remember Sarah came in one morning with this prototype she'd been working on all weekend. She just set her laptop down in the middle of the table, didn't say a word, and we all gathered around. It was the first version of the audio pipeline, completely rough, full of bugs, but you could speak into the microphone and see your words appear on screen about three seconds later. We all just stared at it.

That moment changed everything. Before that, transcription was this abstract problem we were solving on paper. Suddenly it was real. You could talk to a machine and it understood you. Well, mostly understood you. It kept transcribing "neural network" as "neural nutwork," which became a running joke for months.

The hardest part wasn't the technology, honestly. It was convincing people that local transcription mattered. Everyone was so fixated on cloud APIs, sending your audio to some server halfway across the world. We kept saying, look, people care about privacy. People care about latency. People want to own their tools. It took a while, but the world caught up.
